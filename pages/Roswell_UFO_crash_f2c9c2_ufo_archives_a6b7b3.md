## Introduction

That finding does not mean every Roswell-era document survived. Some administrative and communications records from [Roswell Army Air]({{ 'roswell-army-air/' | relative_url }}) Field were destroyed, and the surviving files are scattered across different agencies and archival series. The result is neither a complete documentary vindication of every official claim nor evidence of a hidden alien archive. ## What survives from July 1947?

The most important surviving records are valuable because they were created at the time, rather than reconstructed from memories decades later. In its 1995 investigation, the General Accounting Office—now the Government Accountability Office (GAO)—reported finding two federal records originating in 1947 that directly concerned Roswell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New MexicoJuly 28, 1995 — NSIAD...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

The first was the July 1947 historical report of the combined 509th Bomb Group and Roswell Army Air Field. It recorded that the base had become involved in recovering a “[flying disc]({{ 'flying-disc/' | relative_url }})”, adding that military authorities subsequently determined the object to be a radar-tracking balloon. The entry preserves the same abrupt shift seen in contemporary newspaper coverage: an extraordinary initial description followed by a conventional military explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New MexicoJuly 28, 1995 — NSIAD...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

The second was an FBI teletype sent from the Bureau’s Dallas office on 8 July 1947. According to the message, an Eighth Air Force official told the FBI that the recovered object was hexagonal in appearance, suspended from a large balloon by cable and comparable to a high-altitude weather balloon with a radar reflector. The FBI confirmed the document’s authenticity during the GAO review.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New MexicoJuly 28, 1995 — NSIAD...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

## Why Project Blue Book does not contain a Roswell case file

Project Blue Book is the best-known government archive of UFO investigations, but it is often misunderstood as a complete master file covering every American UFO claim from 1947 onwards. The National Archives holds Blue Book’s declassified administrative records and case files, including reports, photographs, films and supporting material. The Air Force programme and its predecessors recorded 12,618 sightings between 1947 and 1969, of which 701 remained categorised as [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; Nat...</span></span></span>

Roswell, however, does not appear as an investigated case in the Blue Book holdings. The National Archives states that it has been unable to locate documentation concerning the July 1947 [Roswell incident]({{ 'the-1980-book/' | relative_url }}) among those records. This absence matters, but it is not as surprising as it may first appear: the incident was treated in 1947 as the recovery of balloon-related debris, not as an unresolved aerial sighting requiring the later case-processing system associated with Blue Book.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; Nat...</span></span></span>

Blue Book’s files are therefore useful for understanding the wider [military response]({{ 'military-response/' | relative_url }}) to UFO reports, the reporting culture of the early Cold War and the kinds of evidence investigators collected. They are not a hidden Roswell dossier. Searching the collection for Roswell and finding nothing neither proves that no unusual event occurred nor demonstrates that a case file was deliberately removed; it shows that Roswell was not preserved within that particular investigative series.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; Nat...</span></span></span>

This distinction is essential when using government archives. An archive is organised according to the functions and filing practices of the agency that created it. A missing item can reflect classification, destruction, transfer, inconsistent indexing or simply the fact that the event was never entered into that records system.

## The congressional search of the 1990s

The most systematic official search for Roswell records began after New Mexico congressman Steven Schiff sought information from the Department of Defense. Dissatisfied with the responses he received, Schiff asked the GAO to investigate federal recordkeeping and determine whether government agencies held material concerning the 1947 incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF History The Roswell Report</span></span></span>

The inquiry went beyond a search of the Air Force’s public UFO files. GAO contacted or reviewed records associated with the Department of Defense, the Air Force, the Army, the FBI, the Central Intelligence Agency, the National Security Council and other executive bodies. It also examined previously classified material and records concerning reporting requirements for military air accidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New MexicoJuly 28, 1995 — NSIAD...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

GAO’s central conclusion was that the federal search produced no records documenting the recovery of an extraterrestrial vehicle or its occupants. Apart from the two contemporaneous Roswell references, the agencies surveyed supplied no additional 1947 crash documentation. Project Blue Book material and an Air Force statistical study covering early UFO reports likewise contained no Roswell crash or recovery entry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New MexicoJuly 28, 1995 — NSIAD...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

In parallel, the Air Force carried out its own historical investigation. Its 1994 report concluded that the recovered debris was most likely associated with Project Mogul, a classified balloon-based effort intended to detect evidence of Soviet nuclear testing. The published report assembled project histories, technical descriptions, photographs, interviews and other source material used to support that conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF History The Roswell Report</span></span></span>

A second Air Force publication, *[The Roswell Report]({{ 'the-roswell-report/' | relative_url }}): Case Closed*, addressed later stories about recovered bodies. It argued that witnesses had combined or misdated several later military events, including anthropomorphic dummy drops, a 1956 aircraft accident and a 1959 balloon mishap. Critics have challenged this retrospective explanation, particularly because the dummy tests occurred years after 1947, but the relevant archival point is clear: the Air Force located no contemporary record of non-human bodies and instead sought possible sources for the later recollections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/The-Roswell-Report/_bhlid/f4a0c3623ce5085676f0426ec242a4ba91786be7/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[U.S. Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span>

## The destroyed-records dispute

The most consequential gap identified by GAO concerned records from Roswell Army Air Field itself. The inquiry found that outgoing messages from the base covering October 1946 to December 1949 had been destroyed. Certain administrative records of the 509th Bomb Group for the relevant period had also been disposed of.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New MexicoJuly 28, 1995 — NSIAD...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

The surviving disposition documentation did not state precisely who authorised the destruction, when it occurred or which records-management authority justified it. GAO noted that this was not unique to Roswell: an Air Force archivist explained that many organisational records from the period had been destroyed without the governing disposition authority being properly entered on the relevant forms. GAO found similar deficiencies in paperwork relating to the disposal of other Air Force records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New MexicoJuly 28, 1995 — NSIAD...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

For Roswell researchers, this produces an important but limited uncertainty. The destroyed outgoing-message files might have contained routine communications about the recovery, public relations decisions or transport arrangements. They might also have contained nothing beyond administrative traffic. Because the records no longer exist, their contents cannot be reconstructed confidently.

The destruction is therefore evidence of an incomplete archive, not evidence of what the missing pages said. A records gap cannot by itself establish that officials removed proof of extraterrestrial recovery. Equally, it prevents claims that the surviving federal file is a complete minute-by-minute account. The defensible conclusion lies between those positions: part of the relevant administrative record was lost, apparently amid broader weaknesses in historical records management, and its significance remains unknowable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New MexicoJuly 28, 1995 — NSIAD...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

## The FBI files and documents often mistaken for Roswell evidence

The FBI Vault contains 16 parts of UFO-related records documenting the Bureau’s limited involvement in reports from 1947 to 1954. The Air Force was the principal investigating service, while the FBI sometimes received information, checked witnesses or passed reports to military authorities. The Roswell teletype is contained within this larger body of material and is also presented separately by the FBI.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/UFO" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span></span></span>

Not every New Mexico “flying saucer” document in the Vault concerns Roswell. The most notable example is the Guy Hottel memorandum of 22 March 1950, which relayed a third-hand story that three disc-shaped craft and small human-like bodies had been recovered in New Mexico. The document became one of the FBI Vault’s most viewed files, partly because online reports repeatedly linked it to Roswell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/news/stories/ufos-and-the-guy-hottel-memo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufos and the guy hottel memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ufos and the guy hottel memo</span></span></span>

The FBI has explicitly stated that the Hottel memorandum dates from nearly three years after Roswell, that there is no reason to connect the two incidents and that the Bureau did not investigate the reported claim. The memo records what an informant said that another source had reported; it does not certify the story as factual. The Bureau has suggested that it may have repeated a contemporary hoax, although its files do not conclusively establish that origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/news/stories/ufos-and-the-guy-hottel-memo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufos and the guy hottel memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ufos and the guy hottel memo</span></span></span>

This illustrates a recurring problem in UFO archive research: the existence of an official document proves that an agency received or recorded a claim, not that the agency verified it. Intelligence files, FBI memoranda and military correspondence routinely preserve rumours, press reports, public enquiries and unconfirmed testimony alongside investigated material.

## Why the Majestic 12 papers failed archival checks

The so-called Majestic 12 or MJ-12 documents purported to describe a secret group established by President Harry Truman to manage recovered extraterrestrial technology, including material from Roswell. Because they imitate classified government paperwork and name senior officials, they have played a major role in claims that the authentic Roswell archive exists outside ordinary record systems.

The National Archives searched Air Force, Joint Chiefs of Staff, National Security Council and presidential-library holdings for records supporting the documents. Archivists found no authentic file series, references or meeting records establishing the alleged organisation. A supposed memorandum concerning a meeting on 16 July 1954 also conflicted with the surviving National Security Council schedule and administrative record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; Nat...</span></span></span>

GAO separately requested assessments from the Air Force, the Information Security Oversight Office, the National Archives and the Truman and Eisenhower presidential libraries. It reported that those bodies knew the MJ-12 material only because private individuals had submitted copies to them. None found evidence that the papers had originated within the executive branch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAO Breadcrumb Home Reports &amp; Testimonies [Comments On Majestic 12 Material] [Comments on Ma...</span></span></span>

Investigators also encountered a 1980 message containing the words “MJ Twelve”. The Air Force Office of Special Investigations searched for an official record copy and reported that an earlier examination had concluded the message was forged. Although archival silence alone cannot disprove every alleged secret programme, the MJ-12 papers lack the normal provenance—a traceable history of creation, filing and custody—expected of genuine high-level records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO[Comments on Majestic 12 Material] &#124; U.S. GAO Breadcrumb Home Reports &amp; Testimonies [Comments On Majestic 12 Material] [Comments on Ma...</span></span></span>

The National Archives also cautions that certifying a copy simply confirms that it matches an item held in its custody; it does not authenticate the information within that item. An archive may hold a copy because a member of the public submitted it, not because a government office originally created it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; Nat...</span></span></span>

## Where Roswell material is actually held

Roswell-related government material is not contained in one complete “UFO archive”. It is distributed among record groups, agency reading rooms and later investigative collections. The National Archives’ current guide identifies several holdings of particular relevance:

* **Record Group 341**, containing records of Headquarters US Air Force, includes Project Blue Book administrative files, Air Force UFO investigations and the Roswell Report source files compiled between 1987 and 1996.
* **Roswell audiovisual collections** contain moving images and sound recordings assembled during research for the Air Force’s 1990s reports.
* **FBI Vault records** include the 8 July 1947 teletype and wider Bureau correspondence on UFO reports.
* **GAO records** preserve the 1995 congressional investigation, agency responses and findings about destroyed files.
* **Presidential libraries and National Security Council holdings** contain broader UFO-related correspondence but have not produced authenticated documentation of a Roswell recovery programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-collections" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

The “Roswell Report Source Files” are especially easy to misinterpret. They were assembled during the Air Force’s investigation in the late twentieth century; they are not a secret 1947 operational file discovered intact. Their date range and archival context show that they document how the later official report was researched and produced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-collections" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

Likewise, an item appearing in a presidential library may be a citizen’s letter, a press enquiry or correspondence sent to a former president rather than evidence of presidential knowledge. Researchers must examine the creator, date, series description and chain of custody before treating any catalogue result as evidence about Roswell.

## What the new UAP archive changes

The 2024 National Defense Authorization Act required the National Archives and Records Administration to establish a dedicated Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection. The resulting Record Group 615 is designed to receive digital copies of federal records concerning UAP, technologies of unknown origin and non-human intelligence, subject to the disclosure and postponement rules set out in the law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/memos/ac" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

Federal agencies were instructed to review and organise relevant records, prepare digital copies and transfer them to the National Archives. Records are being added on a rolling basis, while agencies continue to manage the original files under their existing retention schedules. Publicly releasable copies are intended to become accessible through the National Archives Catalog; restricted versions may also be transferred with redactions or postponed disclosure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-615" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

This is a significant improvement in centralisation. Earlier UFO records were dispersed across military, intelligence, law-enforcement and presidential collections, often described according to the creating agency rather than under a shared UFO subject heading. Record Group 615 gives future researchers a dedicated finding structure and requires agencies to identify records across different formats.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

It does not, however, mean that a new Roswell disclosure has occurred. The collection is a continuing archival mechanism, not evidence that agencies have transferred records confirming the 1947 recovery of alien technology. Any future Roswell-related release would still need to be judged by its date, provenance, classification history, internal consistency and relationship to already known records.

## How to read the archive without overstating it

Government records are strongest when their evidential role is defined precisely. A contemporaneous teletype can show what one official told another on a particular date. A later investigative report can show which repositories were searched and how an agency interpreted the material it found. Neither automatically supplies an infallible account of the underlying event.

For Roswell, five distinctions help prevent common errors:

1. **A recorded claim is not a verified finding.** The Hottel memorandum proves that the FBI received a story, not that three recovered craft existed.
2. **An unidentified sighting is not necessarily connected to Roswell.** Blue Book’s unresolved cases concern observations that investigators could not satisfactorily explain, not evidence of the Roswell crash.
3. **A missing file is not proof of deliberate concealment.** Records may disappear through routine disposal, poor administration, loss or deliberate destruction; the reason must be established separately.
4. **Declassification does not guarantee completeness.** It opens surviving records that have been reviewed for release, but cannot restore material never created, never filed or already destroyed.

It also documents later official searches, imperfect record preservation and continuing public suspicion. What it does not presently supply is a traceable, contemporaneous documentary chain showing an extraterrestrial craft, non-human occupants or a secret Roswell exploitation programme. ## The enduring archival question

Roswell remains controversial partly because official documentation answers some questions while leaving others structurally open. The surviving [1947 records]({{ '1947-records/' | relative_url }}) support a balloon-related account, and the later Air Force investigation links the debris to Project Mogul. At the same time, the lost Roswell communications files mean the administrative trail is incomplete, while the government’s confusing original public statement gave later suspicions a durable foundation. [gao.gov](https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf)

The most important lesson from the government archives is therefore not that every mystery has been solved or that every absence signals a conspiracy. It is that Roswell must be evaluated through records of unequal quality: a small contemporaneous core, incomplete local files, later official reconstructions, disputed witness testimony and documents whose provenance has failed formal scrutiny.

As Record Group 615 grows, the public may gain easier access to federal UFO and UAP material that was previously scattered across agencies. Unless a future release provides authenticated and independently corroborated Roswell evidence, however, the archival position remains unchanged: the available government record documents secrecy, confusion and imperfect preservation, but not the recovery of an extraterrestrial vehicle.
