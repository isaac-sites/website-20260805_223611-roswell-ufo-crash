## Introduction

The detailed Air Force explanation of Roswell emerged only after Congressman Steven Schiff prompted a government records search nearly five decades later. The resulting reports attributed the recovered debris to Project Mogul, a secret balloon-based effort to detect Soviet nuclear tests, and argued that later stories about bodies had absorbed memories of unrelated military accidents and high-altitude test-dummy operations. Those conclusions remain the principal official account, although gaps in the surviving records and weaknesses in parts of the body explanation continue to fuel dispute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/The-Roswell-Report/_bhlid/f4a0c3623ce5085676f0426ec242a4ba91786be7/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[af.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air ForceThe Roswell ReportThe Roswell Report The Roswell Report CASE CLOSED Air Force News Special ReportThe Roswell Report: Case Closed...</span></span></span>

## Why the early UFO programmes did not solve Roswell

The Air Force’s organised UFO investigations arose from the wider wave of “flying saucer” reports that followed Kenneth Arnold’s highly publicised sighting near Mount Rainier in June 1947. Their central concern was not extraterrestrial life as such, but whether unusual aerial reports indicated hostile aircraft, secret Soviet technology, misidentified American equipment or a danger to national security.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/AARO_Historical_Record_Report_Vol_1_2024.pdf?emci=622cd777-b774-f011-8dc9-6045bda9d96b&amp;emdi=ea000000-0000-0000-0000-000000000001&amp;sourceid=1070813" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[aaro.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">44 Reaper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unclassified Final DSD AARO Historical ReportFebruary 29, 2024 — 44 Predator...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 29, 2024</span></span></span>

Project Sign began in late 1947 or early 1948, depending on how its preliminary activity is dated. It was replaced by Project Grudge in 1949, followed by Project Blue Book in 1952. Investigators gathered witness statements, photographs, radar information, weather data and technical assessments, often using the Air Force Office of Special Investigations to conduct local enquiries. Roswell occupies an awkward place within this history. It occurred during [the 1947 flying]({{ 'the-1947-flying/' | relative_url }})-saucer wave, yet the surviving Sign, Grudge and Blue Book records do not show Roswell being treated as a major UFO case at the time. ## Congress forced a new Roswell records search

The modern Air Force investigation began after Congressman Steven Schiff of New Mexico encountered difficulty obtaining information about Roswell from the Department of Defense. At Schiff’s request, the GAO opened an audit in February 1994 to determine what government records existed and whether records relating to the incident had been handled correctly. The Air Force then established its own declassification and records-review effort within the Office of the Secretary of the Air Force.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; Nat...</span></span></span>

This was not a reopened Project Blue Book case. It was a historical reconstruction conducted approximately 47 years after the event. Researchers searched Air Force records, archival collections, intelligence files, accident reports and material held by other agencies. They also interviewed former personnel and examined records of balloon research, aircraft accidents, parachute testing and other activity in New Mexico.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span>

The GAO’s search produced only two clearly relevant records created in 1947:

* a July 1947 history of the 509th Bomb Group and [Roswell Army Air]({{ 'roswell-army-air/' | relative_url }}) Field, recording the recovery of a “flying disc” later identified by military officials as a radar-tracking balloon;
* an FBI teletype dated 8 July 1947, reporting military information that the recovered object resembled a high-altitude weather balloon fitted with a radar reflector.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

No reviewed agency produced a contemporary document describing extraterrestrial bodies, alien materials or a recovered spacecraft. The GAO also found no evidence that the widely circulated “Majestic 12” papers were authentic executive-branch documents. Federal archivists had located no matching organisation or records, and the Air Force regarded one alleged “MJ Twelve” message as a forgery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/154832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gao.gov.</span></span></span>

The search nevertheless uncovered a genuine records-management problem. Some Roswell Army Air Field administrative records and outgoing messages from the relevant period had been destroyed. The disposition documentation did not state when the destruction occurred or under whose authority it was carried out. The GAO reported the gap but did not conclude that the missing files had contained evidence of a UFO recovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

This distinction is central to interpreting the inquiry. Missing records create uncertainty about routine communications and administration, but they are not positive evidence of extraordinary contents. At the same time, their absence prevents historians from reconstructing every decision made at the base during and after the incident.

## The 1994 report linked the debris to Project Mogul

The Air Force’s first major Roswell publication, commonly called *[The Roswell Report]({{ 'the-roswell-report/' | relative_url }}): Fact versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert*, was completed in 1994. Its principal conclusion was that the debris recovered by personnel from Roswell Army Air Field came from a balloon train associated with Project Mogul rather than an extraterrestrial craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/The-Roswell-Report/_bhlid/f4a0c3623ce5085676f0426ec242a4ba91786be7/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air ForceThe Roswell ReportThe Roswell Report The Roswell Report CASE CLOSED Air Force News Special ReportThe Roswell Report: Case Closed...</span></span></span>

Project Mogul was intended to explore whether high-altitude balloons carrying acoustic equipment could detect distant Soviet nuclear explosions. Although some individual balloon components were ordinary or openly documented, Mogul’s strategic purpose and parts of its operation were classified. The equipment included balloons, lines, instruments and radar reflectors assembled into long trains whose scattered wreckage could appear more extensive and unfamiliar than that of a simple weather balloon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span>

The radar targets are particularly relevant to descriptions of [the Roswell debris]({{ 'the-roswell-debris/' | relative_url }}). They were lightweight structures made from foil, paper, wooden struts and adhesive tape. Project personnel later recalled that some tape carried decorative pink or purple designs originating from the toy-manufacturing materials available to the contractor. That offered a conventional counterpart to later witness descriptions of unusual markings or “hieroglyphics” on parts of the wreckage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span>

The Air Force’s reconstruction also explained why the military might first misidentify the material publicly and then issue a misleadingly simple “weather balloon” account. Personnel at Roswell could recognise balloon and radar-target components without necessarily knowing Mogul’s classified mission. Describing the debris as a weather device protected the underlying nuclear-detection project while avoiding disclosure of its purpose.

Critics have concentrated on the report’s association of the debris with a particular Mogul launch, commonly identified as Flight 4. Documentation of that launch is less direct than records for some other flights, and researchers disagree about whether it was cancelled, launched in a modified form or merely omitted from the formal numbering sequence. The official case therefore rests on a convergence of dates, equipment, geography and witness recollections rather than on a surviving document stating that “Mogul Flight 4 crashed at Roswell”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span>

That limitation does not place an alien craft and a Mogul balloon on equal evidential footing. The archives establish that Mogul equipment was being tested in New Mexico and that its materials resembled those recovered. No comparable physical record establishes an extraterrestrial vehicle. But the lack of a single conclusive chain-of-custody document leaves room for continued argument over the exact launch involved.

## The 1997 report tackled the body stories

The 1994 report dealt mainly with the debris. It did not fully satisfy questions about witnesses who, beginning decades after the incident, described small bodies, military recovery teams, stretchers, sealed containers or activity at a hospital. The Air Force therefore produced a second publication, *The Roswell Report: Case Closed*, in 1997.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/The-Roswell-Report/_bhlid/f4a0c3623ce5085676f0426ec242a4ba91786be7/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air ForceThe Roswell ReportThe Roswell Report The Roswell Report CASE CLOSED Air Force News Special ReportThe Roswell Report: Case Closed...</span></span></span>

Its central argument was that later Roswell narratives had compressed several genuine but unrelated events into July 1947. The report proposed three main sources for the body accounts:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* anthropomorphic test dummies dropped from high-altitude balloons during 1950s research into parachutes and pilot escape systems;
* a 1956 KC-97 aircraft accident in which eleven Air Force personnel were killed;
* a 1959 balloon accident in which two pilots were injured and taken for medical treatment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/The-Roswell-Report/_bhlid/f4a0c3623ce5085676f0426ec242a4ba91786be7/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air ForceThe Roswell ReportThe Roswell Report The Roswell Report CASE CLOSED Air Force News Special ReportThe Roswell Report: Case Closed...</span></span></span>

</div>

The dummy-recovery operations created scenes that could resemble elements of the later Roswell stories. Military and civilian recovery crews used trucks, aircraft and specialised equipment to retrieve human-shaped test figures from remote areas. Damaged dummies could be transported in wooden containers or protective bags, while parachutes, instrument packages and a conspicuous military presence made the operation appear unusual to an uninformed observer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/roswell-aliens-fermi-paradox" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wired.com.</span></span></span>

The report’s most important interpretive claim was not that test dummies fell near Roswell in July 1947. They did not: the relevant dummy programmes occurred years later. Instead, the Air Force argued that memories of 1950s operations had shifted backwards in witnesses’ recollections and merged with the 1947 debris recovery. It described this as “temporal compression”, in which separate events spread over many years become retold as parts of one short episode.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/The-Roswell-Report/_bhlid/f4a0c3623ce5085676f0426ec242a4ba91786be7/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air ForceThe Roswell ReportThe Roswell Report The Roswell Report CASE CLOSED Air Force News Special ReportThe Roswell Report: Case Closed...</span></span></span>

This is also the most contested part of the official explanation. The chronological mismatch makes the dummy theory less direct than the Mogul explanation for the debris. It requires accepting that witnesses misdated events by several years or that later interviewers combined stories from different people and locations. Memory research and the evolution of Roswell testimony make such blending plausible, but the Air Force could not demonstrate the origin of every body claim individually.

The report is strongest where it matches distinctive details—recovery vehicles, parachutes, protective bags and damaged human-like figures—to documented military activity. It is weaker when it implies that one cluster of later operations can explain all testimony about bodies. Some accounts remain too vague, internally inconsistent or late-emerging to be tested conclusively.

## What the investigations established—and what they did not

Taken together, the Air Force and GAO inquiries produced a far more substantial documentary explanation than the brief weather-balloon statement issued in 1947. They established that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* secret balloon research connected with detection of Soviet nuclear tests was active in New Mexico at the relevant time;
* the materials used in balloon trains and radar reflectors closely resembled reported Roswell debris;
* no located contemporary record documented an extraterrestrial craft, alien bodies or recovered non-human technology;
* Roswell was not a prominent case in the surviving files of Projects Sign, Grudge or Blue Book;
* several later “body” details resembled documented Air Force accidents and high-altitude research conducted during the 1950s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

</div>

The investigations did not recover every missing base record, establish a perfect documentary chain from a named Mogul launch to every fragment shown in Fort Worth, or provide a unique explanation for every witness account collected decades later. Nor could a 1990s inquiry recreate with certainty what every officer knew when the original press release was issued.

These limitations help explain why the reports failed to end the controversy. The phrase “case closed” suggested a degree of finality that the surviving evidence could not deliver for every detail. Moreover, the government was effectively asking the public to accept that its 1947 weather-balloon statement was misleading but that its later, more elaborate account was reliable. For readers already persuaded that secrecy implied a cover-up, the correction itself became further evidence of concealment.

Yet uncertainty about particular details is not equivalent to evidence for an alien crash. The official interpretation is supported by documented programmes, identifiable materials and historically verified military operations. The extraterrestrial interpretation depends principally on retrospective testimony, disputed documents and inferences drawn from secrecy or missing records.

## Blue Book’s closure shaped the later Roswell dispute

Project Blue Book ended on 17 December 1969 after an Air Force-sponsored study at the University of Colorado, commonly known as the Condon Report, concluded that further investigation of UFO reports was unlikely to produce major scientific discoveries. The Air Force stated that continued operation was no longer justified on national-security or scientific grounds, and its case files were eventually transferred to the National Archives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; Nat...</span></span></span>

That closure created a lasting contradiction in public perception. Officially, the Air Force had ended systematic UFO investigations before Roswell became a major cultural phenomenon. When the story surged in the late 1970s and 1980s, there was no active Blue Book office to answer new claims or compare them systematically with historical records. Instead, responses came through public-affairs offices, freedom-of-information requests and, eventually, the special 1990s review.

The National Archives now holds extensive Air Force UFO material, including Project Blue Book administrative and case files, Office of Special Investigations records and the source files assembled for the Roswell reports. This allows researchers to inspect much of the documentary foundation rather than relying solely on the Air Force’s summaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/textual-and-microfilm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Records Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Records Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archi</span></span></span>

More recent Department of Defense historical reviews have continued to treat Roswell as an example of classified military activity later interpreted as evidence of extraterrestrial technology. The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office reported in 2024 that it had reviewed official investigations, archives and alleged crash-retrieval claims without finding verified evidence that the US government had recovered or reverse-engineered extraterrestrial craft. That broader conclusion does not independently resolve every Roswell detail, but it is consistent with the Air Force’s archival findings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/AARO_Historical_Record_Report_Vol_1_2024.pdf?emci=622cd777-b774-f011-8dc9-6045bda9d96b&amp;emdi=ea000000-0000-0000-0000-000000000001&amp;sourceid=1070813" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">44 Reaper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unclassified Final DSD AARO Historical ReportFebruary 29, 2024 — 44 Predator...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 29, 2024</span></span></span>

## The lasting lesson of the Air Force inquiries

The Air Force investigations changed the Roswell debate by replacing a simplistic weather-balloon explanation with a documented Cold War setting. Project Mogul offered a credible reason for unusual debris and secrecy; later high-altitude research offered possible sources for some body and recovery stories; and the [GAO search]({{ 'gao-search/' | relative_url }}) showed both the scarcity of contemporary Roswell records and the absence of documentary support for an alien recovery.

Their deeper significance lies in how secrecy, fragmented records and delayed testimony interact. A classified project can generate genuinely confusing observations. An incomplete public explanation can damage trust. Decades later, memories of separate military activities can be reorganised around a famous story, while missing documents make definitive reconstruction harder.

The U.S. Air Force’s record therefore supports neither the idea that officials ignored all unusual aerial reports nor the claim that its investigations uncovered an extraterrestrial Roswell craft. It shows an institution primarily concerned with air defence, foreign technology and classified American programmes, followed many years later by an effort to explain how those same programmes helped create the most enduring UFO controversy of the twentieth century.

    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
