## Introduction

Walter Haut's posthumous affidavit occupies a distinctive place in the Roswell body-recovery debate because it came from the officer who issued the famous 8 July 1947 press release announcing that the Army Air Field had recovered a "flying disc". Unlike earlier Roswell [body stories]({{ 'body-stories/' | relative_url }}), Haut's final affidavit claimed that he personally saw both an unusual craft and small humanoid bodies inside a secure hangar at [Roswell Army Air]({{ 'roswell-army-air/' | relative_url }}) Field. Because the document was signed in 2002 but sealed until after his death in 2005, supporters regard it as unusually candid testimony given without concern for personal consequences. Critics, however, argue that its timing, the evolution of Haut's earlier statements, and the absence of independent corroboration significantly reduce its evidential value. Rather than settling the question of recovered alien bodies, the affidavit has become a case study in how historians and investigators assess late-life testimony against earlier records.

## What the sealed affidavit claimed

The affidavit, signed in December 2002 and first published in *Witness to Roswell* in 2007 after Haut's death, presents a detailed narrative that went far beyond his public role as the base public information officer. According to the document, Colonel William Blanchard personally escorted Haut into a guarded hangar after the initial press release had been issued. There, Haut claimed he observed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* An egg-shaped craft approximately 12–15 feet (about 3.5–4.5 metres) long.
* Metallic debris with unusual physical characteristics.
* Several small bodies, roughly child-sized, partly concealed beneath a tarpaulin.
* Senior officers treating the recovery as an event requiring exceptional secrecy.

</div>

The affidavit concludes with Haut stating that he became convinced he had witnessed "some type of craft and its crew from outer space." Unlike many Roswell body accounts based on hearsay, this document presents itself as direct eyewitness testimony rather than second-hand reporting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Haut" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Walter Haut</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Walter Haut</span></span></span>

Supporters also point to the circumstances of its release. The affidavit was notarised, witnessed, and deliberately withheld until after Haut's death at his request. Advocates argue that this reduced any incentive for publicity or financial gain, while preserving what Haut regarded as his final account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Haut" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Walter Haut</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Walter Haut</span></span></span>

## How Haut's earlier accounts differed

The principal difficulty for historians is not simply whether the affidavit is true or false, but how dramatically it differs from Haut's earlier public statements.

For many years Haut consistently described his wartime role in relatively limited terms. He explained that he drafted and distributed the famous press release on Colonel Blanchard's instructions but generally denied having personally seen either unusual wreckage or bodies. Interviews with researchers during the 1980s and 1990s repeatedly reflected this restrained version of events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2016/03/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: March 2016March 31, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2016</span></span></span>

His 1993 sworn affidavit also stopped well short of the later claims. In that document, Haut expressed [confidence]({{ 'confidence/' | relative_url }}) that Colonel Blanchard and Major Jesse Marcel would not have mistaken ordinary balloon debris for something extraordinary. However, he did not claim to have personally inspected a spacecraft or alien bodies. Instead, he stated only that he had become convinced the recovered material originated from an extraordinary craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Haut" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Walter Haut</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Walter Haut</span></span></span>

The 2002 affidavit therefore represents a significant expansion in both detail and personal involvement. Rather than being the officer who issued an announcement prepared by others, Haut now placed himself inside the secure hangar, observing the alleged craft, bodies and discussions among senior officers.

## Leading interviews and changing details

The sealed affidavit did not emerge in isolation. During his final years Haut participated in interviews with UFO researchers, including recordings that were likewise intended for release only after his death. In these conversations he began discussing matters that he had previously avoided publicly, including attendance at meetings involving senior officers and viewing material inside a base hangar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://military-history.fandom.com/wiki/Walter_Haut" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: military-history.fandom.com">[Military History Fandom]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">military-history.fandom.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Military History Fandom Walter Haut &#124; Military Wiki &#124; Fandom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Military History Fandom Walter Haut &#124; Military Wiki &#124; Fandom</span></span></span>

These later interviews have themselves become part of the credibility debate. Researchers sympathetic to Roswell interpret the progression as evidence that Haut gradually became willing to discuss experiences he had long suppressed because of promises made to Colonel Blanchard.

Other investigators reach a different conclusion. Even Kevin Randle, who spent decades investigating Roswell from a generally pro-investigation perspective, has written that Haut's public story remained limited for decades before expanding substantially in his later years. Randle has also expressed concern that by around 2000 Haut appeared increasingly confused in interviews, making it more difficult to determine which details reflected independent memory and which may have incorporated decades of Roswell literature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2016/03/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: March 2016March 31, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2016</span></span></span>

## How much weight should the affidavit carry?

The affidavit is often presented as one of the strongest body-recovery documents because of Haut's position in 1947. He unquestionably occupied an official role at Roswell Army Air Field and was responsible for distributing the original "flying disc" press release. That gives his testimony greater historical relevance than anonymous or purely second-hand accounts.

Nevertheless, several factors limit its evidential strength.

First, the affidavit was created approximately fifty-five years after the events it describes. Psychological research consistently shows that detailed autobiographical memories can change substantially over such long periods, particularly after repeated discussion and exposure to later narratives.

Second, the affidavit introduces claims absent from Haut's earlier sworn statement and from many decades of interviews. Such substantial expansion requires independent corroboration if historians are to treat it as reliable evidence.

Third, the document has not been supported by contemporaneous military records, photographs, physical evidence, or additional independent witnesses whose accounts clearly match Haut's description of the same hangar scene. The absence of supporting documentation does not by itself disprove the affidavit, but it means the account stands largely on Haut's recollection alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.skeptic.org.uk/2008/07/making-ufology-history-roswell-and-the-story-of-betty-and-barney-hill/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skeptic.org.uk">[skeptic.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skeptic.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skeptic.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why the affidavit remains influential

Despite these limitations, Haut's affidavit continues to occupy a central place in Roswell discussions because it bridges two otherwise separate strands of the story. Haut was not merely another late witness; he was the officer who publicly announced the recovery of a "flying disc" before the Army reversed its position within hours.

For believers, the affidavit explains why the original announcement occurred and why Haut maintained relative silence for decades. For sceptics, it illustrates the hazards of relying on late-life testimony that diverges markedly from earlier documented accounts.

Within the narrower question of [recovered alien body]({{ 'recovered-alien-body/' | relative_url }}) stories, the affidavit therefore carries symbolic importance but limited evidential certainty. It remains one of the most detailed first-person claims associated with Roswell, yet its late appearance, evolving narrative, and lack of independent corroboration mean that historians generally regard it as an important document to analyse rather than decisive proof that alien bodies were recovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.skeptic.org.uk/2022/08/75-years-on-the-roswell-mythology-continues-to-captivate-ufologists-and-the-public-alike/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skeptic.org.uk">[skeptic.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skeptic.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Skeptic...</span></span></span>
