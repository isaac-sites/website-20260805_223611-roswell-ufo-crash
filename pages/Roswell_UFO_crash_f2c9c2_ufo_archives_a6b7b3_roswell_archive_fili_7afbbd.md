## Introduction

One of the most persistent misconceptions about the [Roswell incident]({{ 'the-1980-book/' | relative_url }}) is that, if it were an important government event, there should be a single archive containing every relevant document. That expectation reflects how modern readers think about a historical case, not how governments create and preserve records. In practice, military and civilian archives are organised around the offices that produced records and the functions they performed, not around later historical topics. As a result, documents relating to Roswell are dispersed across multiple record groups, filing systems and repositories, with many routine administrative records never intended to be preserved permanently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/archives-sector/advice-and-guidance/managing-your-collection/archive-basics/introduction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Introduction - Archives sector...</span></span></span>

Understanding this archival mechanism helps explain why the surviving Roswell record appears fragmented. It also provides a more reliable way to interpret what is missing. A document's absence from one collection is not, by itself, [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}) that it was removed or concealed; it may simply have been filed elsewhere, transferred under a different organisational structure, or destroyed under ordinary records-management schedules.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

## Why there is no single Roswell archive

Government archives preserve the records of the organisations that created them. Archivists refer to this as the principle of provenance: records remain associated with their creating office rather than being reorganised into thematic collections after the fact. A military history, an FBI communication, an intelligence memorandum and a logistics record generated during the same week would therefore be preserved in separate archival series because they originated from different agencies and served different administrative purposes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/archives-sector/advice-and-guidance/managing-your-collection/archive-basics/introduction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Introduction - Archives sector...</span></span></span>

For Roswell, this means there was never a central "Roswell file" assembled for future researchers. Instead, any surviving documentation is scattered among:

* unit histories from [Roswell Army Air]({{ 'roswell-army-air/' | relative_url }}) Field;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Army Air Forces administrative records;
* FBI communications;
* intelligence correspondence;
* later Air Force historical investigations;
* congressional and Government Accountability Office (GAO) review files.

</div>

Each collection follows the filing logic of its originating office rather than the logic of the historical event itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

This structure also explains why researchers often encounter apparently isolated documents. A single teletype, diary entry or historical report may survive because it belonged to a record series with permanent historical value, while neighbouring operational paperwork followed a different retention schedule.

## How agencies organise records by function

Military and federal filing systems are primarily administrative tools. They are designed to support ongoing operations, accountability and legal requirements rather than historical reconstruction decades later.

Federal filing manuals classified records according to organisational responsibility, subject categories and office functions. Consequently, records about one incident could be divided among personnel files, operations files, communications, intelligence reporting, public affairs correspondence and historical summaries. These categories frequently ended up in different archival record groups after transfer to the National Archives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/filing-manuals" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Filing Manuals &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Filing Manuals &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/archives-sector/advice-and-guidance/managing-your-collection/archive-basics/introduction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Introduction - Archives sector...</span></span></span> ives itself catalogues holdings in a hierarchy that reflects this origin:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* department or agency;
* organisational division;
* record series;
* individual files or "pieces";
* items within those files.

</div>

Researchers therefore search through the structure of the creating organisation rather than through a master catalogue of historical events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/professional-guidance-and-services/cataloguing-practices/digital-cataloguing-practices/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Our digital cataloguing practices</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesOur digital cataloguing practices - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

Roswell illustrates this system clearly. A document generated by the FBI remained within [FBI records]({{ 'fbi-record/' | relative_url }}). A history written by Roswell Army Air Field became part of Army or Air Force historical collections. Later investigative reports created decades afterwards entered entirely different archival series because they belonged to new inquiries rather than the original 1947 administration.

## Why transfers and indexing create apparent gaps

Archives evolve over decades. Agencies reorganise, responsibilities move, records are transferred between repositories and cataloguing standards change. During each stage, records may become harder to locate without necessarily disappearing.

Several mechanisms contribute to the fragmented appearance of the Roswell record:

**Transfers between agencies.** Records are normally transferred from active offices to records centres before selected material reaches permanent archives. During this process, collections are arranged according to records schedules rather than historical topics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/about/info/whats-a-record.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives What&#x27;s a Record? &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives What&#x27;s a Record? &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

**Changing catalogues.** Older collections often use filing codes or military classification systems unfamiliar to modern researchers. Unless the original filing structure is understood, relevant documents may be overlooked even when they remain publicly available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/filing-manuals" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Filing Manuals &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Filing Manuals &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

**Routine disposal.** Most federal records are never intended to survive permanently. According to the National Archives, only a small percentage of government records are ultimately preserved, while routine administrative material is destroyed according to approved retention schedules.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/about/info/whats-a-record.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives What&#x27;s a Record? &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives What&#x27;s a Record? &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

**Different levels of description.** Some catalogues describe an entire box or series rather than every document inside it. Researchers may therefore need to examine broad collections before discovering individual Roswell-related material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/information-management/manage-information/selection-and-transfer/cataloguing-and-preparation-of-records/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Step 4: Cataloguing and preparation of records</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesStep 4: Cataloguing and preparation of records - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

## Project Blue Book illustrates the filing problem

Project Blue Book is frequently cited as evidence that Roswell documentation has disappeared because the National Archives reports that no Roswell case file has been located within Blue Book's records. However, Blue Book's organisation itself helps explain this absence.

The project investigated reported [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) flying objects from 1947 onwards, and its case files are arranged as Blue Book investigative records rather than as a comprehensive archive of every aviation-related event. The National Archives notes that Roswell documentation has not been located within those files.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

That does not necessarily imply the destruction of a Blue Book case. The incident occurred before Blue Book's mature investigative system existed, and contemporary military authorities concluded that the recovered material was balloon-related rather than an unresolved unidentified flying object requiring long-term case management. Consequently, documents generated during July 1947 would have remained within whatever administrative systems originally handled them instead of automatically becoming part of a later UFO investigation archive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

## How researchers avoid mistaking absence for removal

Professional archival research distinguishes carefully between a missing document and a missing collection. The strongest conclusions come from tracing records through their administrative history rather than searching only by subject.

The GAO's 1994–95 investigation followed this approach. Rather than relying on a single archive, investigators examined multiple agencies, records centres and archival repositories to determine what records existed, how they had been managed and whether any evidence supported claims of improper removal. Their review identified ordinary records-management issues—including the loss of some administrative records—but did not find evidence that files documenting an extraterrestrial recovery had been removed from federal archives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

* Which office would have created the document?
* Was that category of record scheduled for permanent preservation?
* Which archival record group inherited it?
* Has the catalogue changed since transfer?
* Does the absence appear across all relevant repositories or only within one collection?

</div>

These questions focus on archival provenance instead of assuming that every important event should leave behind a complete, unified documentary trail.

## What the filing system tells us about the Roswell record

The fragmented [Roswell archive]({{ 'archive-access/' | relative_url }}) is best understood as a product of government records management rather than as an archive designed around a single historical mystery. Military bases, intelligence offices, law-enforcement agencies and later investigators each created records for their own operational purposes, and those records followed separate preservation paths.

This does not eliminate genuine uncertainties. Some records were routinely destroyed, some have never been located, and others survive only indirectly through later references. However, the structure of federal archives provides a practical explanation for why Roswell documentation is dispersed across multiple collections instead of existing as one comprehensive incident file. Recognising how filing systems work allows researchers to distinguish ordinary archival fragmentation from claims of intentional documentary disappearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/archives-sector/advice-and-guidance/managing-your-collection/archive-basics/introduction/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Introduction - Archives sector...</span></span></span>
