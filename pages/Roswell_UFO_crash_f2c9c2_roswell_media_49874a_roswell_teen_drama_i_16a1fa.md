## Introduction

The [Roswell UFO crash]({{ 'roswell-ufo-crash/' | relative_url }}) entered television as a mystery about hidden extraterrestrials, but its most influential teen dramas transformed that premise into something more intimate: stories about identity, belonging and the emotional cost of living with a secret. Rather than attempting to reconstruct the disputed events of 1947, both *Roswell* (1999–2002) and *Roswell, New Mexico* (2019–2022) treated alien identity as a flexible metaphor for adolescence, social exclusion and the fear of rejection. In doing so, they shifted Roswell from a historical conspiracy into a setting where questions of family, community and self-acceptance could be explored through science fiction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roswell_%28TV_series%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roswell (TV series</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell (TV series</span></span></span>

## From crash mystery to character drama

The original *Roswell* was adapted from Melinda Metz's *Roswell High* novels and centred on three teenagers who survived the Roswell crash and had spent their entire lives concealing their extraterrestrial origins. The UFO incident itself remained largely in the background. Instead, it functioned as the reason why Max, Isabel and Michael could never fully participate in ordinary teenage life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roswell_%28TV_series%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roswell (TV series</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell (TV series</span></span></span>

This marked an important departure from earlier Roswell-inspired fiction. Rather than asking whether the government really possessed alien bodies, the series repeatedly asked questions familiar to many young people:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Who can be trusted with your real identity?
* What happens when family expectations conflict with personal truth?
* Is belonging earned through conformity or honesty?
* Can love survive when one person must hide an essential part of themselves?

</div>

The show's romance between Liz Parker and Max Evans depended less on science-fiction spectacle than on emotional vulnerability. Max's powers and alien heritage mattered because revealing them threatened not only government pursuit but also his friendships, adopted family and future. The Roswell mythology therefore supplied permanent emotional stakes rather than simply an ongoing mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roswell_%28TV_series%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roswell (TV series</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell (TV series</span></span></span>

Television scholars have argued that *Roswell*, alongside series such as *Smallville*, used supernatural identities to explore ordinary adolescent experiences of difference, uncertainty and developing adulthood. The alien became a symbolic way of expressing feelings that many teenagers experience even without science-fiction elements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edinburgh-scholarship-online/book/17751/chapter/175479744" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicTeen programming: isolation, alienation and emerging manhood &#124; Masculinity and Popular Television &#124; Edinburgh Scholarship Onl...</span></span></span>

## Alien identity as a metaphor for concealment

The central metaphor of both Roswell television series is concealment. The alien characters look human, speak like everyone else and participate in everyday life, yet remain constantly aware that exposure could destroy everything they value.

This metaphor works on several levels.

**Adolescence and self-discovery.** Teenagers frequently experience uncertainty about who they are becoming. In *Roswell*, emerging alien abilities coincide with emotional development, making supernatural powers resemble the unsettling changes of adolescence. The characters cannot simply suppress these abilities any more than real teenagers can prevent themselves from growing up.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roswell_%28TV_series%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roswell (TV series</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell (TV series</span></span></span>

**Passing versus authenticity.** The aliens successfully "pass" as ordinary humans, but doing so requires constant vigilance. Everyday interactions become exercises in self-censorship. Friendships deepen only when trusted people are allowed into the secret, making [disclosure]({{ 'disclosure/' | relative_url }}) a recurring emotional turning point.

**Chosen family.** Because only a handful of people know the truth, loyalty becomes more important than biology. Friends gradually become guardians of one another's identities, suggesting that belonging is created through trust rather than shared origins.

**The burden of secrecy.** Unlike many science-fiction heroes who hide powers for strategic reasons, the Roswell characters often conceal themselves because they fear emotional rejection as much as physical danger. Government agencies represent one threat, but losing parents, partners or friends is presented as equally devastating.

This emphasis explains why romance occupies so much of both series. Relationships repeatedly test whether intimacy requires complete honesty and whether love can survive fundamental difference.

## How migration and belonging reshape the setting

The 2019 reimagining, *Roswell, New Mexico*, retained the hidden-alien premise but shifted its social emphasis. Rather than focusing primarily on adolescent identity, it connected alien secrecy with questions of migration, ethnicity and contemporary belonging in the American Southwest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roswell%2C_New_Mexico_%28TV_series%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roswell, New Mexico (TV series</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell, New Mexico (TV series</span></span></span>

The most significant change is Liz Ortecho, reintroduced from the original novels as the daughter of undocumented immigrants. Her experiences with prejudice and uncertainty about legal status deliberately parallel the hidden existence of the alien characters. The series uses the double meaning of the word "alien" to connect extraterrestrial identity with debates over immigration, assimilation and social acceptance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tvguide.com/news/roswell-new-mexico-reboot-cw-review/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tvguide.com">[tvguide.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tvguide.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roswell, New Mexico Review: The Alien Reboot Gets Points for Trying</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TV Guide...</span></span></span>

Rather than presenting Roswell simply as the town of the famous UFO crash, the reboot portrays it as a border community where multiple forms of "otherness" intersect:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* immigrants negotiating acceptance;
* aliens concealing their existence;
* mixed families balancing different identities;
* local institutions that both protect and threaten outsiders.

</div>

Government secrecy therefore acquires a broader meaning. The fear of discovery no longer concerns only military laboratories or conspiracy theories. It also reflects anxieties about documentation, surveillance, discrimination and exclusion from community life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tvguide.com/news/roswell-new-mexico-reboot-cw-review/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tvguide.com">[TVGuide.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tvguide.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roswell, New Mexico Review: The Alien Reboot Gets Points for Trying</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TV Guide...</span></span></span>

The show's ageing of the characters from teenagers to adults also changes the metaphor. ## Why Roswell proved especially suited to stories of belonging

Roswell provides an unusually effective backdrop because its popular mythology already rests on secrecy. The original UFO story revolves around hidden [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}), disputed official accounts and questions about who possesses the truth. Teen dramas redirected those same ideas inward.

Instead of asking whether authorities concealed an alien spacecraft, they asked what it costs individuals to conceal themselves.

That shift allowed familiar science-fiction conventions to support recognisable emotional experiences. Alien powers became symbols of personal difference. Government investigations became expressions of social pressure to conform. The famous Roswell setting supplied an immediate explanation for hidden extraterrestrials while freeing the dramas to concentrate on relationships rather than historical reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roswell_%28TV_series%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roswell (TV series</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell (TV series</span></span></span>

The result is that many viewers now associate Roswell not only with conspiracy theories but also with emotionally driven stories about acceptance, identity and community. In both television series, the enduring mystery of 1947 serves less as the destination than as the narrative foundation for exploring what it means to belong while fearing that revealing one's true self may carry profound personal consequences.
