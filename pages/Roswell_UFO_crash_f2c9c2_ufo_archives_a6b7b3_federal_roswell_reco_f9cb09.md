## Introduction

The federal search for Roswell records in the 1990s was notable not because it uncovered a hidden archive, but because of how broadly it reached across the US government. Prompted by Congressman Steven H. Schiff of New Mexico, the General Accounting Office (now the Government Accountability Office, or GAO) investigated whether official records relating to the July 1947 [Roswell incident]({{ 'the-1980-book/' | relative_url }}) still existed and whether they had been managed according to federal record-keeping rules. Rather than limiting the inquiry to Air Force UFO files, investigators examined records held by military departments, intelligence agencies, executive offices and the National Archives. This unusually wide search reflected the possibility that any significant Roswell documentation might have been dispersed across multiple bureaucracies or remain buried within formerly classified Cold War files.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

## How Wide Was the Federal Roswell Records Search?

The investigation deliberately avoided treating Roswell as simply a UFO case. Instead, the GAO approached it as a records-management question: if an unusual recovery operation had occurred in 1947, what agencies would have created records, where should those records have been preserved, and did they still exist?

## Agencies and Record Groups Examined

The breadth of the review reflected the reality that no single office would necessarily have controlled all documentation relating to a military recovery operation in 1947.

The Department of Defense was the central focus because [Roswell Army Air]({{ 'roswell-army-air/' | relative_url }}) Field belonged to the Army Air Forces, predecessor of the independent US Air Force established later that year. Investigators examined unit histories, command files, administrative correspondence and other operational records. The Air Force simultaneously conducted its own historical research, resulting in *[The Roswell Report]({{ 'the-roswell-report/' | relative_url }}): Fact versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert*, which collected records concerning Project Mogul and other relevant programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/GOVPUB-D301-PURL-LPS71655" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[govinfo.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GOVPUB D301 PURL LPS71655</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Roswell Report: Fact Versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert - GOVPUB-D301-PURL-LPS71655 &#124; Content Details &#124; GovInfoJanuary 1...</span></span></span>

The FBI was included because Bureau agents routinely exchanged information with military authorities on matters affecting national security. The Bureau reported that all Roswell-related material in its possession had already been processed through Freedom of Information Act requests. The most significant surviving item was the well-known 8 July 1947 Dallas office teletype describing a balloon-like object with a radar reflector.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

The CIA was searched despite not existing at the time of the incident—it formally began operations in September 1947—because later intelligence files could conceivably have incorporated retrospective analyses or transferred records concerning Roswell. Executive agencies including the National Security Council, the Department of Energy and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy likewise reported that they held no government records relating to the Roswell crash. ## Why Previously Classified Files Were Included

One distinctive feature of the inquiry was its willingness to examine both classified and unclassified records.

By the early 1990s, researchers had argued that if Roswell involved a secret military project—or an alleged recovery concealed for national security reasons—the most relevant documentation might never have appeared in ordinary administrative files. The GAO therefore reviewed classified and formerly classified records dating from the late 1940s and early Cold War rather than relying solely on publicly available collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

This approach also reflected another possibility: records might not mention "Roswell" at all. Documents concerning radar experiments, balloon programmes, intelligence reporting or command communications could potentially illuminate the event without using later terminology associated with UFO investigations.

The Air Force's own research similarly incorporated previously classified information about Project Mogul, whose mission to detect Soviet nuclear tests had remained secret for decades. According to the Air Force, understanding that programme explained why some personnel in 1947 could not openly describe the recovered debris even though most surviving Mogul technical records themselves were not classified by the 1990s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/The-Roswell-Report/quot/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[af.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Roswell Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Roswell Report</span></span></span>

## What the Multi-Agency Search Still Could Not Recover

Despite the unusually broad search, investigators encountered significant archival limitations.

The most important finding was not the discovery of hidden Roswell files but the absence of many records that would normally have documented routine activities at Roswell Army Air Field. The GAO determined that some administrative records covering base operations had been destroyed. In several instances investigators could establish that records no longer existed but could not determine precisely when they had been destroyed or under what disposal authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

Investigators also found no air accident report because none had been required under the regulations applicable to the recovery of what military authorities identified as balloon debris. The absence of such a report therefore could not, by itself, be treated as evidence of concealment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

No surviving government record documented the recovery of an extraterrestrial spacecraft, non-human bodies or other extraordinary materials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

## What the Agency Search Demonstrated

The significance of the 1990s investigation lies less in what it found than in the scope of the search itself. Rather than accepting or rejecting Roswell claims on the basis of one military archive, investigators traced potential records across defence organisations, intelligence agencies, executive offices and archival repositories.

The inquiry demonstrated that the federal government did not rely exclusively on Project Blue Book or later UFO collections when examining Roswell. Instead, it attempted to reconstruct the documentary trail wherever surviving records might reasonably have been expected. The outcome was a mixed one: investigators confirmed that a genuine cross-government search had been conducted and that archival losses had occurred, but they did not uncover evidence of a surviving multi-agency cache documenting an extraterrestrial recovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>
