## Introduction

The missing Roswell records remain controversial because they create a genuine gap in the historical record without proving why that gap exists. During a congressional inquiry in the 1990s, investigators discovered that key [Roswell Army Air]({{ 'roswell-army-air/' | relative_url }}) Field (RAAF) administrative files and outgoing message logs covering July 1947 had been destroyed. The surviving paperwork documenting that destruction was itself incomplete, leaving no clear record of who authorised the disposal, when it occurred or under which records-management authority it took place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/Roswell_UFO_crash_f2c9c2_roswell_report_90b4db_missing_roswell_reco_354737-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Missing Records illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Roswell_UFO_crash_f2c9c2_roswell_report_90b4db_missing_roswell_reco_354737-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Roswell_UFO_crash_f2c9c2_roswell_report_90b4db_missing_roswell_reco_354737-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

For critics of the official explanation, these missing files raise the possibility that important [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}) disappeared. For most historians, however, the absence of records is not evidence that they contained proof of an extraterrestrial craft. Instead, it represents an archival problem: missing documentation limits what can be reconstructed about the events of July 1947, but it does not establish what those destroyed records would have shown.

## Which Roswell records were destroyed?

The controversy centres on two categories of records that would normally have documented routine activity at Roswell Army Air Field:

* **Administrative records** covering approximately March 1945 through December 1949.
* **Outgoing message files** covering approximately October 1946 through December 1949.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

These were not files labelled "Roswell UFO" or "[flying disc]({{ 'flying-disc/' | relative_url }})". Instead, they were routine military records that could have contained orders, correspondence, daily administration and communications relating to the base's activities during the period that included July 1947.

When the U.S. General Accounting Office (now the Government Accountability Office, or GAO) investigated the matter at the request of Representative Steven Schiff, it found that these collections no longer existed. At the same time, investigators located other contemporary records that had survived, including the July 1947 history prepared by the combined 509th Bomb Group and Roswell Army Air Field, as well as the FBI's 8 July 1947 teletype describing recovery of an object resembling a balloon with a radar reflector.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

The existence of these surviving records demonstrates that the historical archive was incomplete rather than entirely erased.

## Why the disposal paperwork became controversial

The missing records attracted particular attention because the documentation explaining their destruction was itself inadequate.

The GAO reported that the available records-disposition form did not identify:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* which organisation or individual destroyed the files;
* when the destruction occurred; or
* under what legal or administrative authority the destruction took place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

</div>

Those omissions are significant from an archival perspective because government records are normally destroyed according to approved retention schedules. Without complete disposal documentation, later investigators cannot determine whether the destruction followed routine procedures, reflected poor record-keeping practices or resulted from some other administrative failure.

Importantly, the GAO did **not** conclude that the records had been deliberately removed to conceal evidence of an alien spacecraft. Instead, it documented an irregularity in the surviving paperwork governing records disposal.

## What historians can—and cannot—infer from the gap

The missing files unquestionably reduce historians' ability to reconstruct exactly what occurred at Roswell during July 1947. Had the administrative files and outgoing messages survived, they might have clarified questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* internal communications immediately following the debris recovery;
* the sequence of military reporting;
* logistical movements around the base; and
* how information was transmitted to higher headquarters.

</div>

Because those records no longer exist, such questions cannot always be answered from primary documentation.

That limitation, however, does not justify treating every later claim as equally probable. Historical method distinguishes between **[missing evidence]({{ 'missing-evidence/' | relative_url }})** and **positive evidence**. A destroyed archive means some questions cannot be answered conclusively; it does not demonstrate that the destroyed material supported any particular theory.

In practice, historians therefore rely on the surviving documentary record, contemporary newspapers, FBI communications, military histories, witness testimony and later official investigations while recognising that the absence of some base records leaves unavoidable uncertainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

## Why missing records do not automatically imply a cover-up

One of the most persistent misunderstandings is the assumption that destroyed records necessarily indicate intentional concealment.

Several considerations complicate that conclusion.

First, many military records from the mid-twentieth century were routinely destroyed under records-retention policies that were less comprehensive than modern archival standards. Administrative files were often viewed as temporary rather than permanently valuable.

Second, the Air Force informed the GAO that, under regulations in effect during July 1947, there was no requirement to prepare a permanent accident report for the recovery of what officials considered a weather balloon. By contrast, permanent reporting requirements applied to military aircraft accidents, and the GAO identified several such reports from New Mexico during the same month that had been retained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

Third, although the disposal paperwork was incomplete, investigators found no documentary evidence demonstrating that the files had been selectively removed because they contained extraordinary information.

These factors do not eliminate suspicion for every observer, but they explain why mainstream historical scholarship regards the missing records as an unresolved archival issue rather than direct proof of an organised cover-up.

## Why the controversy persists

The destroyed Roswell records continue to feature prominently in debates because they leave room for competing interpretations.

Supporters of a cover-up hypothesis argue that missing communications could have documented an event later concealed by the military. Since the original files no longer exist, they contend that the official explanation can never be fully verified.

Historians generally adopt a more cautious position. They acknowledge that the archival gap is real and that incomplete disposal documentation is an unusual and legitimate point of concern. At the same time, they note that historical conclusions must rest on surviving evidence rather than speculation about documents that no longer exist. The surviving [1947 records]({{ '1947-records/' | relative_url }}) recovered by investigators do not describe an extraterrestrial craft, and no newly discovered primary document has filled the archival gap with evidence supporting such a claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

The result is a genuine historical ambiguity. Missing records prevent a perfectly complete reconstruction of events, ensuring that some questions about Roswell will remain unanswered. Yet the existence of that gap, by itself, does not establish that officials destroyed evidence of alien technology. The controversy endures because the absence of records creates uncertainty—but uncertainty is not the same thing as proof.
