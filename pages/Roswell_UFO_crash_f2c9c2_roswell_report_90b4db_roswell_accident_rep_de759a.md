## Introduction

One question examined during the official Roswell investigations was why no permanent Army aircraft accident report exists for the July 1947 incident. At first glance, the absence of such a report has been cited by some as [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}) that records were removed or concealed. However, the General Accounting Office (now the Government Accountability Office, or GAO) approached the issue from a records-management perspective rather than by asking what crashed. Its conclusion was narrower: if the recovered object was treated as a weather balloon or balloon-borne equipment, the Army's 1947 aircraft accident reporting rules did not require the preparation of a formal aircraft accident report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

## What 1947 accident rules required

The GAO reviewed the Army Air Forces' reporting requirements that governed aviation accidents in 1947. According to Army records officials, formal air accident reports were required for aircraft accidents and those reports were to be retained permanently. Consequently, had a military aeroplane crashed near Roswell, investigators would normally expect a permanent accident record to exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

To test that expectation, the GAO searched surviving records from July 1947. It identified four Army Air Forces aircraft accidents in New Mexico during that month, all of which involved military aircraft and all of which occurred after the initial Roswell press release on 8 July 1947. The Navy reported no air accidents in New Mexico during the same period. These findings demonstrated that the reporting system was functioning and that genuine aircraft accidents were being documented through the established process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

The question therefore became whether the Roswell recovery belonged in that category at all.

## Why balloon incidents followed different reporting rules

The Air Force advised the GAO that weather balloon crashes were not subject to the same reporting requirements as aircraft accidents. In other words, if personnel regarded the recovered object as a balloon or balloon-borne device rather than an aeroplane, existing regulations did not require preparation of a permanent aircraft accident report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

Supporting memoranda gathered during the 1994 Air Force investigation reinforced this administrative distinction. One internal memorandum stated that weather personnel were not required to record ordinary weather balloon landings and that only meteorological data were routinely retained. Researchers also reported finding no weather archive documenting a Roswell balloon crash during the relevant period. Rather than indicating misconduct, this reflected normal record-keeping practice for balloon operations, which generated far less formal documentation than aircraft accidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dafhistory.af.mil/Portals/16/documents/AFD-101201-038.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dafhistory.af.mil">[DAF History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dafhistory.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DAF History The Roswell Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DAF HistoryThe Roswell ReportDecember 1, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2010</span></span></span>

This difference became especially significant because the Air Force's 1994 explanation identified the debris as belonging to Project Mogul, a classified balloon programme rather than a conventional aircraft. Although Project Mogul itself remained secret in 1947, the administrative handling of balloon recoveries would still have followed balloon-related procedures rather than aircraft accident regulations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/roswell.pdf?ver=2017-05-22-113531-807" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ROSWELL REPORT Fact versus Fiction in the...</span></span></span>

## How nearby New Mexico crashes were ruled out

Another part of the GAO's review was determining whether the Roswell event might have been confused with a documented aircraft accident elsewhere in New Mexico.

The investigators compared known accident records with the timing of the [Roswell incident]({{ 'the-1980-book/' | relative_url }}). The four recorded Army Air Forces accidents all involved identifiable military aircraft, occurred after the Roswell announcement and were separately documented. None corresponded to the debris recovered on the Foster Ranch or matched the circumstances described in contemporary Roswell records. Likewise, the Navy reported no aviation accidents in New Mexico during July 1947 that could account for the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

This comparison served an important administrative purpose. It showed that the missing Roswell aircraft accident report was not explained by misfiling under another known crash investigation. ## What the absence of an accident report does—and does not—show


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The lack of a formal aircraft accident report has often featured in debates about Roswell, but the GAO treated it as a question of regulatory procedure rather than proof of any particular explanation.

</div>

Its investigation reached three related findings:

* Aircraft accidents in 1947 were subject to permanent reporting requirements.
* Weather balloon incidents were not governed by those same rules.
* Therefore, if the recovered object was handled administratively as a balloon, the absence of an aircraft accident report would be expected rather than unusual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/gao/nsi95187.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramGAO - Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico...</span></span></span>

The GAO also acknowledged a separate issue: some [Roswell Army Air]({{ 'roswell-army-air/' | relative_url }}) Field administrative records and outgoing message files from the period had been destroyed under circumstances that could not later be fully reconstructed. That destruction complicated historical research, but it did not alter the specific conclusion regarding aircraft accident reporting requirements. The two questions—missing administrative files and the absence of an aircraft accident report—were treated as distinct issues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

Within the broader Roswell Report investigations, this administrative analysis addressed a common misconception. The official position was not that every crash involving airborne equipment should have produced an aircraft accident file. Rather, it was that balloon recoveries and aircraft crashes followed different reporting systems, making the lack of a Roswell aircraft accident report consistent with the military's stated classification of the recovered debris.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>
